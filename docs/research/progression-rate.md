# Rate of progression: research notes

Part B of [block-length-and-progression.md](./block-length-and-progression.md), which has the bottom line for both parts and how the sources were read.

## B. Rate of progression

### B1. Guidelines

- **ACSM 2009, PMID 19204579.**
  - Abstract: "it is recommended that 2–10% increase in load be applied when the individual can perform the current workload for one to two repetitions over the desired number."
  - Body text, evidence category B: "2–10% (lower percent for small muscle mass exercises, higher percent increase for large muscle mass exercises) ... on two consecutive training sessions."
  - Volume "may be gradually increased (e.g., 2.5–5%)".
  - Frequency: 2–3 days per week for novices, 3–4 for intermediates, 4–5 for advanced.
  - Confidence: **primary**.
  - The 2002 version of the stand (PMID 11828249) has the same 2–10% rule. Confidence: **primary**.
- **ACSM 2026 overview of reviews, PMID 41843416.**
  - Recommends "progressive RT" but its abstract gives no new increment rule.
  - Confidence: **primary**. I did not read the full text for an updated increment.
- **NSCA "2-for-2 rule"**:
  - The rule: if the lifter completes 2 or more reps over the goal on the last set in 2 consecutive workouts, increase the load.
  - Commonly cited increments:
    - novices, upper body: 2.5–5 lb (about 1–2.5 kg)
    - novices, lower body: 5–10 lb (about 2.5–5 kg)
    - larger or more experienced lifters: 5–10+ lb upper and 10–15+ lb lower
  - Sources: US Coast Guard column (https://www.mycg.uscg.mil/News/Article/2910464/) and CSCS exam-prep sites. These come from the NSCA *Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning* textbook, which I did not read.
  - Confidence: **secondary, UNCONFIRMED against NSCA text**.
  - The rule matches ACSM's "1–2 reps over, two consecutive sessions" trigger, which *is* confirmed.

### B2. What Nippard says

- **Two progression modes:**
  - Video: "How To Train For Pure Muscle Growth", 2023-10-29.
  - Rep progression: "three sets of 10 in week 1, three sets of 11 in week 2, three sets of 12 in week three" (moderate to high reps), then "add some weight and return back to 10 reps".
  - Load progression at a fixed rep count: "add 10 pounds to each side each week" (low to moderate reps). That is an illustrative squat example, about +9 kg per week total, not a prescription.
  - Technique improvements also count.
  - Confidence: **primary (via transcript)**.
- **By training status:**
  - Video: "How To Make Gains At Every Fitness Level", 2022-05-29.
  - Beginners: "aiming to add some amount of weight to them each and every week at the same rep count".
  - Intermediates: "you can't simply add weight to the bar or the machine each and every workout anymore without compromising technique". Instead, "add one rep each week rather than increasing the weight". Adding sets is the "last straw".
  - Expected muscle gain:
    - beginner male: about 10–25 lb (4.5–11 kg) over the first 1–2 years
    - intermediate: another 10–20 lb
    - advanced male: 1–2 lb per year
  - Confidence: **primary (via transcript)**.
- **Compound vs isolation:**
  - Video: "How I Blew Up My Shoulders", 2025-12-01.
  - Heavy press: 5–6 reps, "add a little weight each week" (5 or 10 lb weekly, per the transcript summary).
  - Isolation: "If you start with 15s and just add 5 lbs each week, by week eight you'd be up to 50 lb laterals. That linear progression won't work". Use double progression (for example 10–12 reps).
  - "you don't need to add a rep to all three sets. Just one rep to one set is enough to keep progression moving, especially as you get more advanced."
  - Confidence: **primary (via transcript)**.
- **General:** "add a little weight or a rep each week" (2025-05-04 and 2026-01-13 videos). In the 2026 video his app shows which plates to add.
  - I found **no explicit micro-loading or % figure** from Nippard. He uses "a little weight". **UNCONFIRMED** whether his paid guides specify kg increments. The third-party "2.5 kg upper / 5 kg lower" figures are low-confidence.
- **Tracking:** track at least the heavy compounds ("you can track your squats presses and heavy pulls and then kind of wing it for the lighter isolation stuff"). Source: Sulek video, 2024. Confidence: **primary (via transcript)**.

### B3. How fast strength actually rises (by training status)

- **Untrained, short term:** Nóbrega et al. 2023, J Strength Cond Res. **PMID 36515591**.
  - Untrained men, 24 sessions to failure.
  - Volume load progressed **2.30 ± 0.58% per session** with a rep-zone (RM-zone) model, against 1.01 ± 0.55% with a %1RM model.
  - The faster volume-load progression went with more CSA gain.
  - Confidence: **primary**.
- **First years:** Steele et al. 2023, Res Q Exerc Sport. **PMID 35591809**.
  - 14,690 people on minimal-dose training (1 set to failure, 1× per week, 6 exercises).
  - About **30–50% strength gain in year 1**, a practical plateau by about 1–2 years, and about 50–60% above baseline at 6 years.
  - Confidence: **primary**. The low dose may understate what a higher-volume program achieves.
- **Advanced:** Latella et al. 2020, J Strength Cond Res. **PMID 32865942**.
  - 1,897 powerlifters over 15 years.
  - Total rose by about **0.15 kg per day** (about 55 kg per year) for men with a mean baseline total of about 513 kg.
  - The strongest quartile gained 0.102 kg per day against 0.211 kg per day for the weakest.
  - Confidence: **primary**.
- **Dose-response by status:** Rhea et al. 2003, Med Sci Sports Exerc. **PMID 12618576**.
  - Untrained lifters gain most at about 60% 1RM and 3 days per week per muscle. Trained lifters gain most at about 80% 1RM and 2 days per week.
  - Confidence: **primary**.
- The often-quoted "about 40% untrained, 20% moderately trained, 16% trained, 10% advanced, 2% elite" strength-gain figures, attributed to ACSM 2002 or Kraemer & Ratamess 2004, are **UNCONFIRMED**. They are not in either abstract or the 2009 text, and I could not access the 2002 full text.
- **Reps vs load:** Plotkin et al. 2022, PeerJ. **PMID 36199287**.
  - Trained lifters, 8 weeks.
  - Adding reps at a fixed load and adding load at fixed reps gave similar hypertrophy and strength. Hypertrophy modestly favoured reps; 1RM slightly favoured load.
  - Both are valid progressions, so an app can accept either as "progress".
  - Confidence: **primary**.

### B4. Injury risk from progressing too fast

- **No resistance-training-specific evidence found.**
  - No acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR) study in weight training or powerlifting appeared in a Europe PMC title search.
  - The ACWR concept comes from team-sport running loads and is itself methodologically disputed. Not verified here.
- **Aasa et al. 2017**, Br J Sports Med. **PMID 27707741**.
  - Powerlifting injury incidence was 1.0–4.4 per 1000 training hours, which the authors call similar to other non-contact sports.
  - "Only one retrospective study had analysed possible risk factors."
  - Confidence: **primary**.
- **2024 update**, BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med. **PMID 39650568**. Same incidence ranges. No load-progression risk factors reported in the abstract. Confidence: **primary**.
- **Narrative review 2018**, PMID 30057777.
  - Suggested causes include "excessively heavy loads ... insufficient resting times ... faulty lifting technique".
  - This is hypothesis, not tested.
  - Confidence: **primary, but narrative/low-evidence**.
- **Implication:** any app cap, such as warning above a 10% jump or when reps fall below the range, is a conservative heuristic grounded in ACSM's 2–10% band and Nippard's "without compromising technique". It is not an injury threshold from data.
